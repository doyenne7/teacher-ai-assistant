How AI Makes Decisions
     Teacher resources                Printing Options
Dive into the exciting world of artificial intelligence with Code.org’s innovative module, “How AI Makes Decisions,”
tailored for upper elementary school students! This module is named to highlight its focus on unraveling the
processes that AI systems use to analyze training data and make decisions. In these five lessons, AI concepts are
broken down into engaging, age-appropriate lessons that focus on the critical role of data. Students will use
Code.org’s AI tools for fun, educational AI experiences while gaining insight into how AI works behind the scenes.
Each lesson provides several opportunities for class discussions about AI, bias, and the impact of AI tools on daily
life.

     Lesson 1: Making Predictions

    This lesson will introduce students to the fundamentals of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning by
    focusing on predictive data models. Students will get hands-on practice with supervised and reinforcement
    learning. They’ll start with data that has already been sorted and progress to making predictions on their own
    of how to sort items on screen. In the final level, students will try their hand at sorting data on their own and
    will enlist a partner to predict how the items have been categorized.

    This hands-on experience will be complemented by discussions on the significance of data categorization
    and the challenges posed by limited datasets. Students will explore a real-world example where AI
    predictions have been used to help humans. The lesson aims to demystify AI technology, emphasizing its
    pattern-based logic rather than perceiving it as magical or random.

 Lesson 2: Training AI

In this lesson, students will learn about how the inputs they give a machine learning model directly impact
the predictions that the model makes. First, they will learn about the difference between random predictions
and AI predictions, before training their own AI model to make predictions on whether an item is a fish or
trash in the ocean. Finally, students will discuss the implications of biased training data by discussing
hypothetical scenarios.

 Lesson 3: Using AI Bot

In this lesson, students will begin thinking about how to classify real world objects by their features. They will
build out two feature vectors (foods and emojis) in order to learn how AI makes decisions based on the
probability of an outcome. Students will use “Dance Party: AI Edition” to see first-hand how AI can make
tasks more efficient, but that it might come at the cost of customization. Students will consider what it
means to collaborate and co-create with AI.

 Lesson 4: AI Tools in School

In this lesson, students will evaluate sample student data. They will choose a hypothetical AI tool and
consider what training data the tool will need to successfully make a decision. Then, students will write a
letter to the school principal explaining their opinion on using AI tools. At the end of the lesson, students will
reflect on what they have learned about AI over the course of the 4 lessons.

Students will understand that a predictive AI model is only as good as the information it was trained on.
Therefore it is important to train the model on a large amount of data that is relevant to the task at hand.
Also, students should recognize that there needs to be human oversight when it comes to working with AI
to ensure that any mistakes made by AI get corrected.

 Lesson 5: Share Your Voice

In this lesson, students will create an artifact explaining their opinion on using AI tools. They have the option
to write a letter to the principal, make a speech or song, make a poster, create a Sprite Lab project, or code a
poem to share their opinion. At the end of the lesson, students will reflect on what they have learned about
AI over the course of the module.

[Optional] Context Setting (10 minutes)
 Display: Show “What is AI?” slide

Do This: As an optional general introduction to AI, show students this short video.

Goal: Some important and relevant points to clarify before beginning this module include: AI stands for Artificial
Intelligence. An AI machine is simply a computer that can make decisions after learning from data given to it by
humans. A few real world examples of AI include: voice assistants like Siri or Alexa, self driving cars, and robots.

 Display: Show “Discuss” slide

Discuss:

    What is this AI robot doing?
    How do you think it learned how to do this?
    What do you think would happen if you put a red block on the table?
    What are some things this robot probably can not do?

Discussion Goal: Students should notice that this robot has likely been trained how to sort between blue and green
blocks. If it has not been taught how to sort red blocks it might incorrectly sort them into the blue or green items or
leave it on the table. This robot likely cannot perform tasks unless they involve human-like motor skills. For example,
this robot likely cannot generate text, speak, or see. This is a simple example of AI that directly relates to the
activities students will be doing in upcoming lessons.


    Teaching Tip                   

 In this lesson, students will be sorting items by different features. You may want to demonstrate how to do this
 by using physical manipulatives before students complete the levels online. You can use items that you have in
 your classroom or print and cut out the sorting manipulatives that students will see in the following levels.

Warm Up

 Display: Show “Warm Up” slide

Do This: Generate a selection of random books using the Random Books Generator site. Show students the 6
books that were generated, and explain that these recommendations are randomly selected from an existing list of
books. Then, search for a student’s favorite book on Amazon and scroll down to look at Items to "Explore." For
example, check out "More items" to explore on this search for Diary of a Wimpy Kid. If students aren’t familiar with
Amazon, ask them what types of videos are recommended for them when they finish watching a video on YouTube.

Discuss:
    How did each site decide which books to feature?
    Which set of books are you more likely to read?
    How do you think these book recommendations might be different from ones given to your friend, parent,
    teacher, or librarian?

Discussion Goal: Students should be able to explain that the Random Book Generator site generates books
randomly from an unsorted/untagged list of books. If you were to keep clicking “Generate,” it would pick 6 books
randomly from the list each time.

Context: Amazon collects data associated with each book. For example, it tracks what other books were bought by
people who bought the book that the user searched for. It also uses a recommendation algorithm to look for similar
books (author, grade level, content) to the book being searched for. Searching for Diary of a Wimpy Kid on Amazon
results in recommendations for other books in the Wimpy Kid series.

Students will likely find books in the random book generator that are not at the right reading level for them or that
they don’t find interesting. In comparison, Amazon might recommend more familiar or relevant books. They should
understand that if their parent/friend/teacher/librarian clicks “Generate” on the random book generator they are
equally likely to get the same book recommendations. However, if their parent/friend/teacher/librarian were to type
in their own favorite book into Amazon’s search, they would get more customized recommendations.

AI for Oceans (20 minutes)

 Display: Show “Machine Learning” video

Discuss: What does the speaker mean when she says computers learn through “trial and error” and “lots of
practice”?

Discussion Goal: Have students reflect on the previous lesson. Sometimes they made a guess about what box to
put an item in and they were incorrect. They received feedback, then corrected their guess, and moved on to make
a prediction about the next item. This is an example of “trial and error” or reinforcement learning. A machine learning
model gets fed data, makes predictions, and then gets feedback based on how accurate those predictions were in
order to make better predictions in the future.

Discuss: How do you think this Bot is able to tell the difference between cars and bikes?

Discussion Goal: Students should be able to explain that this bot was trained on many different images which were
tagged as either a bicycle or a car. Some features that it might be looking for: 2 or 4 wheels, how much of the
background is visible behind the image, windows or no windows, handlebars or no handlebars.

Turn and Talk (5 minutes)

 Display: Show “Turn and Talk” slide

Discuss:

    Why might your AI predictions look different from your classmate’s AI predictions even though you trained AI
    on the same word?
    What features were most important to you when you were labeling fish?

Discussion Goal: Students should be able to explain that their training dataset reflects their own opinions. Students
might have different opinions about which fish looks “Fast” or “Happy” because these words are subjective,
meaning they could be different for each person. Additionally, students might have chosen to look at certain
features of a fish and not others, such as if it was showing its teeth or if its eyebrows were raised. AI makes
predictions based on its training data, so students with different training datasets will likely notice different AI
predictions.


Reflect (5 minutes)

 Display: Show “Reflect” slide

Discuss:
    What would happen if all the fish labeled 'Angry' were blue and all the fish labeled 'Awesome' were red?
    What issues might that cause in the long run?

Discussion Goal: If students were to label only blue fish as “angry” and only red fish as “awesome”, the computer
would learn that biased pattern and might start labeling all blue fish as “angry” or all the red fish as “awesome” even
when they're not. These inaccurate and unfair predictions, also called bias, could cause issues in the future if the
computer learns something untrue about a particular type of fish. We can help solve this problem, by giving the
computer training data collected from a variety of sources and making sure the data represents all types of fish.



Wrap Up (5 minutes)

Question of the Day

 Display: Show “Question of the Day” slide

Discuss: How do computers become more accurate at making predictions?

Discussion Goal: Students should understand that the more data they give AI, the more accurate its predictions
are: They should understand that tagging data can be subjective. For example, students might have different
opinions about which fish look “silly”. Getting data from all the students in the class helps eliminate bias when using
training data that might be based on opinions. Students should consider multiple scenarios and not make any
assumptions about what AI knows. For example, AI doesn’t know that otters and dolphins belong in the ocean if it
was only trained on fish. Students will get more practice doing this in the Wrap Up question.


Wrap Up

 Display: Show “Wrap Up” slide

Pretend your school is using Attendance Bot, an AI device that scans the classroom and checks if a student is
present or absent.

If a student is sitting in their seat at 9:00 am, they are marked Present. Otherwise, they are marked Absent and their
teacher, principal, and parent is notified.

    What are the pros and cons of using Attendance Bot?
    What could cause Attendance Bot to mark a Present student as Absent?
    What should the makers of Attendance Bot consider when training their AI?

Discussion Goal: Have students consider that this AI device may not always be more accurate than a teacher
taking attendance because it can only see the class at one specific moment in time (con). On the flip side, it might
make a teacher’s life easier because it automates a task that a teacher would have to do by hand (pro). Students
might also consider how the AI tool can’t ask questions or see nuanced scenarios, such as a student who is present
but not in their seat. Have students consider examples of when Attendance Bot might be wrong where a human
would not be.

Examples of when Attendance Bot might mark a present student as absent:

    If the student is present but somewhere else in the classroom, e.g. at the teacher’s desk, passing out papers, in
    another seat
    If the student is kneeling in their chair or bending down to pick something off the floor
    If a student is in the bathroom
    If the student is in a wheelchair or uses a mobility device that prevents them from being able to sit in the
    school chair

The makers of Attendance Bot should consider the different scenarios above when training their AI. They might
also want to have the Bot take attendance throughout the day to make sure that it is more accurate.

Wrap Up (5 minutes)

Question of the Day

 Display: Show “Question of the Day” slide

Discuss: Why might AI give different answers to the same question?

For example, I asked AI what colors are in a sunset. It told me, “Orange and red”. When my classmate asked the AI
it said, “Purple and pink”.

Discussion Goal: Make sure students understand that AI is trained on billions of data points representing many
different opinions. Since not everyone agrees on for example, what colors are in a sunset, AI will make a prediction
based on the probabilities of different responses. If most people say that a sunset contains the colors orange and
red, then AI will usually say “orange and red”. But since sometimes people see purple and pink in a sunset, AI will
occasionally say “purple and pink.” Just as asking different people what colors are present in a sunset will yield
different answers, asking AI the same question may result in different responses.

Have students reflect on the Warm Up activity in which AI Pet Picker chose a rat, when we were expecting it to
choose a puppy. Point out how each Pet Shop employee had different opinions about how cute each animal is and
how big it is. Sometimes conflicting opinions in the training data can lead to conflicting AI predictions. If AI Pet
Picker was trained on the opinions of everyone in the world, it might not pick the pet that you or I had in mind, but it
would certainly reflect someone’s opinion from the training data.

Additionally, you can point out that there can be small and large effects of AI making a different or even incorrect
prediction. In the previous lesson, if AI couldn’t correctly predict that a starfish belonged in the ocean, it would
assume that it was trash which needed to be removed. In the real world, this could cause large negative
environmental impacts.

 Display: Show “Wrap Up” slide

Discuss: Choose one of the following jobs: Veterinarian, Teacher, Bus Driver, Artist.

Now, imagine if the person doing this job were replaced by an AI robot.

    Would you want AI to replace a human in this job? Why or why not?
    What would happen if this AI got something wrong?

Discussion Goal: Students should be able to give examples of what might happen if this AI got something wrong:
misdiagnosis, misinformation, taking a wrong turn, etc. The takeaway here should be that AI can help us automate
tasks so we can do them faster, e.g. pick out background effects, but AI needs oversight. There should be a
partnership between humans and AI so that we are not entrusting AI to make the final decision or to operate
unchecked.

    Teaching Tip                  

 The following slides present two options for completing this warm up. You can either have students answer the

 paper (Paper Version). For both options, you have the choice to use sample student data if you don’t have

Say: We’re going to collect some data of our own. In a minute, you will go to Level 1 and answer the four multiple

Discuss: Project your class responses on the board. Ask students to look at the data and answer the following four

   . What does each row represent?
   . What information is captured in each column?
   . What decisions could you make using this dataset?
   . Would you be able to make a decision for the whole school using just this data?

Discussion Goal: Students should understand that each row represents the results of one survey question. Each
column represents how many students chose that response (represented as a percentage of the entire class).
Some decisions that could be made based on this dataset: what flavors of ice cream or pizza to buy for a class
party, whether recess should be inside or outside, what colors to use for classroom decorations or class folders.
This data shouldn’t be used to make a decision on behalf of the entire school because it’s biased towards the
opinions of one classroom. Students should keep in mind any bias that could result from a dataset having limited or
subjective data.

Question of the Day

 Display: Show “Question of the Day” slide

Discuss: How can you prevent bias in your training data?

Discussion Goal: Students should understand that training data can be subjective, for example what an “angry” fish
looks like or someone’s favorite ice cream flavor. It is important to consider what data should and should not be
collected. When having AI decide where to seat people in the cafeteria, including what languages they speak is
very helpful. On the flip side, certain data which may seem irrelevant, such as the number of detentions a student
received, and could even lead AI to make a biased decision. If this is the case, this data should likely be left out of
the training dataset and/or AI’s predictions should be evaluated by a human.

the data it was provided. It is a problem because kids might be looking for indoor activities and / or not live near the
beach. In order to make this AI tool useful for all kids, we should train it on more data that includes indoor activities
and activities that aren’t location-specific.
